# 2. Literature Review

This chapter reviews both the academic literature and the existing systems relevant to the seating allocation problem defined in Chapter 1. ## 2.1 Literature Search Strategy

The literature reviewed in this chapter was identified through a two (2) stage search.

In the first stage, database searches were conducted on Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore, the ACM Digital Library and Scopus. The keywords used were combinations of *seating allocation*, *seat assignment*, *spectator allocation*, *office space allocation*, *constraint optimization*, *constraint programming*, *CP-SAT*, *minimal perturbation*, *reallocation stability* and *large neighbourhood search*, and the search was restricted to publications in English. A work was included when it either addressed the assignment of people to physical seats, or presented a constraint optimization or repair technique that is transferable to that problem. A work was excluded when it addressed the apportionment of seat counts to groups rather than the assignment of individuals to physical seats, when it addressed revenue management or overbooking rather than the allocation of seats that have already been committed, or when it addressed pedestrian flow and evacuation simulation rather than seat assignment.

In the second stage, backward snowballing was applied to the reference lists of the most directly relevant works found in the first stage, in particular Ipsen et al. (2026), in order to locate earlier seating allocation studies that the keyword search did not return.

The search established that the literature which treats seating allocation as an optimization problem is limited when compared against the general combinatorial assignment literature, and that the work which does exist is distributed across three (3) loosely connected streams, which are office space allocation, legislative and assembly seat allocation, and event or venue spectator seating, rather than forming a single coherent research area. This dispersion is itself relevant to this project, because it means that no single established problem formulation can be adopted directly for the PJKIT case study.

For the existing systems reviewed in Section 2.4, a separate review was conducted across five (5) categories of solution that an event administrator in PJKIT's position could plausibly adopt, which are manual practice, enterprise event management, dedicated seating planners with automatic assignment, consumer seating chart tools, and adjacent systems that allocate numbered seats under rules in other domains. One (1) or two (2) representative products were examined in each category, and every assessment is based on the vendor's publicly available product documentation and feature pages inspected in June 2026.

## 2.2 Academic Studies on Seating and Space Allocation

Beyond the commercial event planning software reviewed in Section 2.4, a series of academic studies addresses seating and spectator allocation as a combinatorial optimization problem. Five (5) works are reviewed in this section, followed by the primary technical literature on the solver selected for this project.

**Hierarchical office seating allocation.** Ipsen et al. (2026) formalize the Hierarchical Seating Allocation Problem, in which organizationally nested teams must be assigned to seats on an office floor plan so that hierarchically related teams are seated near one another. They decompose the problem into per-level sub-problems solved from the top down, and they compare four (4) sub-problem solvers, which are an exact integer program that selects a central seat for each team and minimizes the distance from the team members to it, an iterative clustering heuristic that resembles k-means, a regret-based greedy baseline, and a warm-started local search that re-optimizes only the seats near an existing incumbent solution.

Three (3) findings from this study transfer to the present project. First, exact optimization remains tractable at the sub-problem scale, but only when the problem is deliberately decomposed. The demand-driven tier bands of PJKIT play a comparable decomposition role, and this project exploits them by creating decision variables only for the participant-seat combinations that the participant's tier band permits. Second, their warm-started local search demonstrates that re-optimizing a bounded region around an incumbent solution is the practical mechanism for keeping successive seating plans stable, which is the same principle that the incremental repair mechanism in Section 1.5.5 applies. Third, their qualitative evaluation shows that a solution which has been proven optimal against the stated objective may still differ from the arrangement that a human decision maker would choose, which is the reason that this project retains the review of the event administrator before publication rather than publishing the solver output directly.

The limitation of this study for the present purpose is that its objective is the proximity of related teams on an unstructured floor plan. It contains no notion of a fixed row and position geometry, no multi-seat allocation unit, no ordering among individuals, and no mechanism for repairing a seating plan that has already been communicated to the people it affects. Their local search improves a solution within a single planning run, and it does not preserve a commitment that has already been published.

**Legislative and assembly seat allocation.** Hales and García (2019) address the allocation of physical seats to parties in a legislative chamber using mathematical optimization, and it is from this formulation that Ipsen et al. adapt their own integer program. Among the reviewed works this is contextually the closest to the PJKIT case study, because a legislative chamber shares the essential features of a community assembly hall, which are a fixed geometry of rows and seats, groups that are entitled to blocks of adjacent seating, and a status ordering that determines which blocks are the more desirable ones. It differs from the PJKIT case in that group membership rather than individual contribution amount drives the placement, and in that no ordering constraint among individuals of the kind that PJKIT applies within each tier is present.

**Spectator allocation for massive events.** Muñoz et al. (2006) address ticket-to-seat allocation for a Formula 1 Grand Prix at a scale of up to 50,000 seats. In their formulation the ticket groups carry a category, a priority rank and a dispersion flag, while the seats carry a zone, a row, a category, a rank and a status. Their rules are divided explicitly into mandatory rules, under which a ticket group must be assigned seats of a matching category and status, and nine (9) optional rules which cover subgroup splitting, rank alignment between the ticket and the seat, the minimum and maximum number of tickets in a row, never leaving a single ticket isolated, avoiding empty seats at the edges of rows, and distributing occupancy evenly so that a partially sold venue still appears full. Their solver adapts the region growing technique from computer vision image segmentation, in which a seed seat is selected for each ticket subgroup and is then grown outward by iteratively taking the best scoring neighbouring seat, after which a local search pass improves the first candidate solution. The method is an anytime method, which means that a usable solution appears within minutes and improves if more time is allowed.

Two (2) aspects of this study are directly relevant. The first is that their division between mandatory and optional rules independently arrives at the same hard and soft constraint architecture that this project adopts, which supports the position that the distinction is intrinsic to the seating domain rather than an artifact of one modelling choice. Their optional rules also identified a class of constraint that had not been considered during the initial elicitation for PJKIT, which is the class of rules that govern row-level packing and the distribution of vacant seats. Comparison against the historical 2023 and 2024 seating maps of PJKIT then determined the specific form that this class takes at PJKIT. Rather than avoiding isolated seats and edge gaps, PJKIT packs each row outward from the centre aisle and fills the numbered section from front to back with no vacant seat, which is recorded as constraints C15 and C16 in Section 3.5.3. The literature therefore supplied the constraint category, and the empirical validation against the historical seating maps determined its content.

The second aspect is methodological. Region growing is a constructive heuristic, and it offers no completeness or optimality guarantee, it reports no solver status from which the event administrator could judge whether a better seating map exists, and it encodes its rules inside a growth scoring function rather than declaratively, so that changing a rule requires changing the algorithm. For a system whose event administrator must defend seating decisions to a community, the absence of any falsifiable quality claim is disqualifying, and the method is therefore reviewed as a comparison point in Section 2.7 rather than adopted.

**Distance-constrained venue seating.** Barry et al. (2021) and Stoll (2022) address seat allocation in fixed-row venues under distance constraints that arise from public health separation requirements. Their relevance to this project is not the distancing objective, which does not apply to PJKIT, but their demonstration that adjacency and separation requirements over a fixed row and position geometry can be expressed and solved as explicit optimization constraints. This is the structural feature that PJKIT shares through its two-seat Emperor allocation units and its centre aisle restriction, and these two works are the closest published treatments of that specific geometry.

**Aisle-interrupted row geometry.** Sun (2020) contributes a combinatorial model rather than an allocation method, treating stadium rows that are interrupted periodically by access aisles as an arithmetic sequence with a periodic jump, and deriving closed-form expressions for the number of seats in each row and for the cumulative capacity. The PJKIT venue is simpler, as it comprises sixteen (16) uniform rows of sixteen (16) seats with eight (8) seats on each side of a single centre aisle. The model of Sun nevertheless corroborates, from an independent mathematical direction, this project's treatment of the centre aisle as a structural break in the row geometry rather than as an ordinary gap between two consecutive seats, which is the property that underlies the hard rule that physical adjacency is computed over physical seat positions and that no Emperor pair may span positions 8 and 9.

**Adjacent problem class.** For the completeness of the review scope, the Office Space Allocation problem (Ülker, 2013; Awadallah et al., 2012) allocates people or entities to rooms and floors subject to space and adjacency requirements, and is typically solved using metaheuristics. It is distinguished from seating allocation in that it allocates areas rather than individual numbered seats, and it is noted here in order to bound what this project is not addressing.

**Primary literature on the selected solver.** The technology selection in this project does not rest on vendor documentation alone. The architecture of CP-SAT is described in the peer-reviewed literature by its own development team. Perron et al. (2023) document that CP-SAT is a purely integral constraint programming solver built on the lazy clause generation paradigm, which is the hybrid of SAT-style conflict-driven clause learning and constraint propagation solving introduced by Stuckey (2010), and that it is augmented with an integrated linear programming relaxation for bounding and with a portfolio of diverse parallel search workers. Under lazy clause generation, the integer variables and constraints are represented natively by propagators, and the SAT clauses that explain each propagation or conflict are generated on demand, so that a failed search branch yields a reusable learned clause rather than a blind backtrack. This architecture is what allows constraints such as the tier band capacity, the Emperor pairing and the contribution ordering to be expressed natively as integer and Boolean constraints, without hand-compiled linearization, while still permitting the solver to prove optimality and to report an explicit status on instances of the size of this case study (Perron et al., 2023; Stuckey, 2010; Google, n.d.-a).

**Research gap.** None of the reviewed studies provides the combined capability that the PJKIT case study requires. Ipsen et al. (2026) and Hales and García (2019) achieve exact optimization, but through bespoke formulations that are built for one venue and one rule set, with no configurable rule layer and no mechanism for repairing a seating map that has already been published to participants. Muñoz et al. (2006) achieve scale and anytime behaviour, but with no optimality or completeness guarantee and no explicit solver status. Barry et al. (2021) and Stoll (2022) treat adjacency constraints over a fixed row geometry, but under a single distancing objective rather than a configurable multi-criteria one. Sun (2020) models the venue geometry but does not address allocation at all. None of the reviewed works models a multi-seat allocation unit, a demand-driven tier band with a shareable boundary row, or a contribution ordering among individuals within the same tier, and none of them treats the repair of an already-published plan as a problem in its own right.

This project therefore does not claim that no solution exists anywhere for any part of this problem. The claim that this review supports is narrower and is stated in that narrower form throughout this chapter, which is that **none of the reviewed academic studies provides the combined capability that the PJKIT case study requires**, and that this project is intended to combine a general-purpose constraint solver, a provable optimality claim with explicit status reporting, and a stability-preserving reallocation mechanism within one system.

## 2.3 Stability-Preserving Reallocation and Repair-Based Optimization

Objective 2 concerns a different problem from Objective 1. Once a seating map has been published and communicated to participants, a subsequent participant data change does not call for a new seating map but for a repair of the existing one, in which the assignments that have already been announced are preserved wherever possible. Two (2) established literatures address exactly this situation, and together they supply the theoretical basis for the incremental repair mechanism described in Section 1.5.5.

**The minimal perturbation problem.** Given a solution to a constraint satisfaction problem and a subsequent change to that problem, the minimal perturbation problem seeks a new solution that differs from the original in as few assignments as possible. El Sakkout and Wallace (2000) formalize this problem for dynamic scheduling, and the line of work by Müller, Rudová and Barták applies it to course timetabling, in which an already-published timetable must absorb a late change while disrupting as few existing commitments as possible. Course timetabling is a close structural analogue of the PJKIT case study, because in both situations a plan has been published to the people it governs, a change then arrives, and the cost of the repair is measured primarily by how many previously communicated assignments are disturbed rather than by the abstract quality of the new plan. This is the literature that justifies why the reallocation objective in this project ranks the number of unaffected participants moved above the ordinary seating preference penalties, rather than treating movement as one soft criterion among equals.

**Large neighbourhood search.** The technique of freezing most of an incumbent solution and re-optimizing only a bounded neighbourhood around the region of interest originates with Shaw (1998) in constraint programming approaches to vehicle routing, and it is surveyed by Pisinger and Ropke (2010). This is precisely the mechanism by which the reallocation in this project operates. When a participant data change occurs, the unaffected assignments are locked, the affected seats are released, and a reduced constraint model is constructed over only the affected participants and a limited set of candidate seats, with the neighbourhood expanded progressively if no feasible repair is found within it. Naming the technique matters for two (2) reasons. It establishes that the escalation strategy described in Section 1.5.5 is a recognized method with known behaviour rather than a heuristic invented for this project, and it explains why the repair model is expected to be substantially smaller and faster than the initial generation model, since the repair model may involve only a handful of allocation units and candidate seats rather than the 94 allocation units and 232 assignable seats of the full problem.

**Solution reuse in dynamic constraint satisfaction.** Verfaillie and Schiex (1994) address the more general question of how a previous solution may be reused when the constraints of a problem change, and they provide the earlier framing within which both of the above techniques sit.

**Switching costs in dynamic constraint optimization.** Hoang (2022) formulates the Proactive Dynamic Distributed Constraint Optimization Problem, whose objective over a sequence of time steps comprises a utility term for each step less a weighted switching cost term that penalizes any change to the value of a decision variable between two consecutive steps, together with bounds on the loss of quality incurred by a finite horizon approximation. The requirement of PJKIT to repair a published seating map while minimizing seat movement is a single-step instance of this switching cost structure, which places the movement minimization term of constraint C9 within a formally analysed framework rather than leaving it as an ad hoc heuristic. Two (2) limitations bound the transfer of this work. The setting of Hoang is distributed, with autonomous agents that coordinate by message passing, whereas the engine in this project performs a single centralized solve within one function invocation, and his continuous-domain extensions do not apply to a finite discrete set of seats. The structure of the objective is therefore adopted, while the solution architecture is not.

Taken together, these four (4) works establish that the stability-preserving repair of an existing assignment is a studied problem with established formulations, and that the mechanism proposed for Objective 2, which is a bounded repair neighbourhood with a movement-dominant objective and progressive expansion upon infeasibility, is a domain-specific instantiation of that body of work. What is absent from the reviewed literature, and what this project contributes, is the application of these techniques to seating allocation specifically, where the repaired artifact is a public commitment to named individuals and where the structural constraints include multi-seat allocation units and tier ordering.

Third, there is a **controlled dynamic reallocation gap**. Neither is equivalent to a workflow in which the changed participant data is submitted, a repair is computed against the published seating map under a movement-minimizing objective, a new version is produced together with a movement summary, and only an approved version is published. Configurable constraint weights also receive little explicit support, which leaves the event administrator unable to formally adjust the relative importance of competing allocation criteria, and most systems offer no measurable quality metric by which to judge whether a generated seating map is balanced and defensible.

Fourth, and most decisively, **none of the reviewed systems can express the PJKIT-specific structural rules at all**. This is a categorical finding rather than a graded one. A two-seat allocation unit that must occupy a valid within-row pair and must never be separated by the centre aisle, a tier band whose row boundaries are derived from the registration demand of each event and which may be shared between two adjacent tiers, and an ordering rule which guarantees that no higher-contributing participant is seated behind a lower-contributing participant of the same tier, are not configurable in any of the reviewed products.

Among the reviewed commercial systems, PerfectTablePlan is one of the more sophisticated, as it supports automatic seating assignment and employs a genetic algorithm to search for strong layouts once the combinatorial space outgrows exhaustive search (Oryx Digital Ltd., n.d.). Its automatic assignment nevertheless does not deliver explicit fairness metrics, participant-profile-based constraint weighting, or dynamic weighted constraint optimization as the central objective of the system.

A white space therefore persists for a system that unites automated seating allocation with configurable constraints and measurable evaluation of the generated result. The research gap is a **combination gap** rather than a single missing feature, and this project is intended to address it with an intelligent seating allocation service that draws on participant profile data, predefined constraint templates, configurable soft-constraint weights and measurable quality indicators in order to generate seating maps. It is intended, in effect, to be not merely a visual seating chart tool but a decision-support system for fair and constraint-compliant allocation.

## 2.6 Constraint Modelling and the Computational Character of the Problem

### 2.6.1 Constraint modelling and rule conversion

The proposed system requires the human-readable event seating rules to be converted into a structured mathematical model before an optimization solver can process them, an activity which is known as constraint modelling or constraint formulation. Constraint modelling translates a real-world problem into formal components, which are the decision variables, the domains, the constraints and the objective function (Rossi et al., 2006). In this project the real-world problem is the assignment of event participants to available seats under participant-profile and event-specific rules. The decision variables represent the unknown assignment, which is the seat assigned to an individual participant or the seat pair assigned to an Emperor registration. The domain of each variable is the set of permissible seat identifiers or valid pairs. The constraints encode both validity and preference, which are that each participant receives exactly one allocation unit, that each seat serves at most one occupant, that unavailable seats are excluded, that Emperor pairs never straddle the centre aisle, that the tier bands and the contribution ordering are respected, and that participants of higher priority occupy the more suitable seats.

The proposed system distinguishes **hard constraints**, which are the mandatory rules that a valid seating map must satisfy, from **soft constraints**, which are the preferences that are satisfied as far as possible. The hard constraints comprise one allocation unit per participant, at most one occupant per seat, exclusion of the structurally blocked seats in the centre of the hall, valid seat range, status eligibility, side-of-hall accessibility placement, demand-driven tier-band placement ordered Emperor → Merit → Bodhi with a boundary row shareable between two adjacent tiers, valid Emperor pairing, within-tier contribution ordering, and the front-to-back packing and centre-out fill of the numbered section. The soft constraints comprise the priority-score alignment, the activeness score, the category suitability, and the movement minimization upon reallocation. This distinction follows the valued constraint satisfaction and constraint optimization approaches, in which preferences, costs and priorities are represented and optimized rather than treated as strict pass-or-fail conditions (Schiex et al., 1995).

A weighted-sum aggregation of the soft-constraint penalties is adopted rather than a lexicographic or a max-min aggregation, for three (3) reasons. The PJKIT stakeholders described the trade-offs among the soft factors as situational rather than strictly ranked, and stated that no single factor is always the most important one, as recorded in Appendix B under references E16 and E17, which rules out a lexicographic ordering. The relative weights are an input that the event administrator configures, and they are to be calibrated with the stakeholders through the monthly validation visits described in Section 4.2. A single scalar objective is also required by the solver.

Three (3) modelling principles from the constraint programming literature are directly relevant. **Linearization**, including Boolean linearization, expresses complex logical conditions using integer or Boolean variables, which is essential for CP-SAT because it requires all constraints and objective coefficients to be integers (Google, n.d.-a). Accordingly, every penalty value and weight in this project is defined on an integer scale, and any fractional cost is scaled to an integer before the model is constructed. **Reification** links the truth value of a logical condition to a Boolean variable, so that for example a Boolean indicator represents whether a participant has moved from their previous seat after a reallocation, which is what enables movement to be counted and penalized in the objective. **Channeling** links different layers of decision variables, for instance by connecting the assigned seat identifier of a participant to the row, physical position, tier band and priority rank of that seat, and the OR-Tools documentation describes channeling constraints as the standard mechanism for representing such relationships, commonly implemented through implications or half-reified linear constraints (Google, n.d.-b).

A fourth modelling decision is specific to this domain and follows from the venue geometry, which is that **the physical seat position and the seat priority rank are distinct attributes and must never be conflated**. The adjacency and aisle rules operate on the physical position, whereas the preference alignment operates on the priority rank. The two differ because desirability at PJKIT increases toward the centre aisle rather than monotonically along a row, as recorded in Appendix B under reference B6, so that the most desirable positions are those nearest the aisle while the pair structure simultaneously forbids treating positions 8 and 9 as adjacent. The mathematical basis for treating the aisle as a structural break rather than as an ordinary gap is the model of aisle-interrupted row geometry given by Sun (2020).

### 2.6.2 Why the problem requires optimization rather than enumeration

Two (2) distinct claims must be separated here, because they are frequently conflated and only one of them establishes the need for a constraint solver.

The first claim concerns the **infeasibility of manual verification**. Even in the simplified case in which every participant occupies a single seat, assigning 150 participants into the 232 assignable seats of the PJKIT layout admits 232!/(232 − 150)! ordered assignments, which is approximately 8.75 × 10^326 possible combinations. No event administrator working by hand can survey a space of this size, and none can demonstrate that a manually produced seating map is the best available arrangement under the community's own stated rules. This is a scale at which exhaustive checking is impractical and at which nested-loop, if-else programming becomes difficult to maintain, slow to search, and weak at arbitrating conflicting preferences. This claim directly substantiates Problem Statement 1, and it is the correct use of the combinatorial count.

The second claim concerns **computational hardness**, and a large search space alone does not establish it. The classical linear assignment problem, in which *n* individuals are assigned to *n* seats and the cost of each assignment is independent of every other assignment, also admits a factorial number of candidate solutions, and it is nonetheless solvable in polynomial time by the Hungarian algorithm (Kuhn, 1955). A factorial search space is therefore not by itself evidence that a general-purpose solver is required.

What makes the PJKIT problem computationally hard is its **structure**, which places it outside the linear assignment class in four (4) ways. An Emperor registration consumes an adjacent pair of seats subject to position-dependent validity, which makes the problem one of matching and packing rather than one of one-to-one assignment; in the validated scenario the 112 Emperor participants form 56 such pairs among the 94 allocation units. The within-tier contribution ordering couples participants to one another, so that the cost of an assignment is no longer separable for each participant-seat pair, which is precisely the condition that the Hungarian method requires. The tier-band boundaries are derived from the registration demand of each event and may be shared between two adjacent tiers, so the band structure is itself dependent on the decisions being made rather than being fixed input. Finally, the front-to-back packing and centre-out fill rules impose global contiguity conditions rather than costs on individual assignments.

Ipsen et al. (2026) establish the relevant complexity result for this class of problem, observing that the seating allocation problem is a special case of the capacitated p-median problem, which is NP-hard (Mu & Tong, 2019). Consequently no exact polynomial-time algorithm is expected for the PJKIT problem, and the practical alternatives are either a heuristic without a quality guarantee or an exact solver that is capable of proving optimality on instances of this size. This project adopts the latter, and Section 2.7 compares the candidates.

## 2.7 Comparison of Seating Allocation Methods

Based on the research study, multiple algorithmic approaches could be implemented for the creation of the seating allocation solver function. Table 2.3 compares the reviewed seating allocation methods from the previous studies.

**Table 2.3 Comparison of seating allocation methods**

| Method | How It Works | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Manual assignment | The event administrator assigns seats by judgement against a printed or spreadsheet layout. | Flexible and requires no tooling. | Slow, inconsistent between allocators, unverifiable against the stated rules, and requires rework on every change. |
| First-come-first-served | Seats are assigned in registration order. | Trivial to implement. | Ignores the tier, the contribution amount and the seat suitability entirely. |
| Random allocation | Seats are assigned at random among the eligible seats. | Useful as a lower bound during evaluation. | Observes no event rule. |
| Greedy priority-based | Participants are sorted by priority and each is assigned the best available seat one by one. | More rule-aware than random or first-come-first-served, and approximates the current manual practice. | Early locally good decisions can force poor later assignments; weak with competing constraints; cannot recover from a dead end. |
| Region growing with local search (Muñoz et al., 2006) | Seeds each participant group at a seat and grows the block outward by taking the best scoring neighbour, then improves the result with local search. | Anytime behaviour, and demonstrated at a scale of 50,000 seats. | Constructive heuristic with no optimality or completeness guarantee; no explicit solver status; the rules are embedded in the growth scoring function rather than declared, so a change of rule requires a change of algorithm. |
| Genetic Algorithm (GA) | Evolves a population of seating plans through selection, crossover and mutation. | Flexible for large search spaces and complex scoring, and used in seating tools such as PerfectTablePlan (Oryx Digital Ltd., n.d.). | Typically cannot prove mathematical optimality; requires tuning; provides no falsifiable status reporting; and results vary between runs, which conflicts with the reproducibility requirement in NFR8. |
| Integer Linear Programming / Mixed-Integer Programming (ILP/MIP) | Represents the problem with linear equations, integer variables and an objective function. | Mature exact optimization with strong solvers when the model is linear (Williams, 2013), and used for the seating sub-problems of Ipsen et al. (2026) and by Hales and García (2019). | Both ILP and CP-SAT require integer modelling, so that cost is common to either choice. The differential cost is that the logical and conditional rules, which for PJKIT are the pair validity, the aisle exclusion, the contiguous filling and the conditional eligibility, must be compiled manually into linear form, typically through big-M constructions that are error-prone to author and that weaken the relaxation on which the solver relies for bounding. There is no native reification or channeling. |
| Constraint Programming — Satisfiability (CP-SAT) | Represents the problem with integer variables, native logical constraints, Boolean indicators and an objective function, and solves it through lazy clause generation with conflict-driven clause learning, linear-relaxation bounding and portfolio search (Stuckey, 2010; Perron et al., 2023). | Native reification and channeling for if-then rules with no big-M compilation; proves optimality and reports an explicit status, which is OPTIMAL, FEASIBLE, INFEASIBLE, MODEL_INVALID or UNKNOWN; deterministic and reproducible; production-grade and free. | Requires careful integer-based modelling, and optimality may be claimed only when the status returned is OPTIMAL. |

CP-SAT is not claimed to be universally superior to mixed-integer programming. Both are exact methods, both require integer modelling, and for a problem whose constraints were purely linear a well-formulated MIP model would be an equally defensible choice. CP-SAT is selected because of the particular character of the PJKIT rule set, in which the pair validity, the aisle exclusion, the tier-band membership with a shareable boundary row, the within-tier ordering and the contiguity of filling are all logical rather than arithmetic conditions.

CP-SAT, implemented through Google OR-Tools, is therefore selected because the proposed system must do more than assign seats. It must enforce the hard structural rules, which are the two-seat pairs, the aisle breaks, the tier bands and the ordering, it must optimize the weighted preferences, it must preserve stability during reallocation, and it must report measurable allocation quality. Relative to ILP and MIP, which are the closest exact alternatives, CP-SAT expresses the conditional and structural rules of PJKIT natively through reification and channeling rather than through hand-compiled big-M linearizations, while retaining exactness, and its lazy-clause-generation architecture with conflict learning, linear-programming bounding and parallel portfolio search is specifically designed to prune search spaces of this character (Perron et al., 2023; Stuckey, 2010).

Relative to the published seating heuristics, which are region growing and the genetic algorithm, CP-SAT provides two (2) properties that neither can offer. The first is a provable optimality claim, and the second is an explicit and falsifiable solver status that the system surfaces as part of the allocation quality. Under the standing rule of this project a seating map is never represented as optimal unless CP-SAT returns OPTIMAL, and a FEASIBLE result that is reached at the time budget is reported as OPTIMAL_NOT_PROVEN in accordance with FR7 (Google, n.d.-a). Determinism matters equally, because NFR8 requires identical output for identical input, which a stochastic population-based method cannot guarantee. The greedy method is retained as an evaluation baseline that represents the current manual practice, and the genetic algorithm is noted as a literature benchmark and a possible future comparison.

## 2.8 Literature Synthesis Against the Three Problem Statements

By synthesizing the reviewed academic studies and the reviewed existing systems against the three (3) problem statements defined in Section 1.3, it can be confirmed that this project is positioned in the correct area, and that **none of the reviewed academic studies or existing market systems resolves the three problem statements for the PJKIT use case**.

**Problem Statement 1, which is the inconsistency and inefficiency of multi-criteria manual seating allocation, is not resolved by any reviewed solution.** The reviewed commercial systems provide varying levels of guest management and visual seating, but none of them supplies a configurable rule layer over the domain attributes of PJKIT, which are the contribution amount, the activeness, the tier and the participation status, and none of them can express the PJKIT structural rules at all, as Table 2.2 establishes categorically. The reviewed academic studies supply exact optimization only in bespoke and non-configurable formulations that are built for one venue and one rule set (Ipsen et al., 2026; Hales & García, 2019), or heuristic methods that provide no quality guarantee and no falsifiable status from which the event administrator could judge the result (Muñoz et al., 2006). Neither category therefore allows a seating map to be produced consistently, repeated identically, or defended against the community's own stated rules. The proposed system is designed to address this through configurable hard and weighted soft constraints solved by a general-purpose exact solver, with the intention of making the allocation consistent between allocators, reproducible across runs, and explainable through a per-constraint penalty breakdown, which will be evaluated against the criteria in NFR1 and FR16.

**Problem Statement 2, which is the high operational disruption and the absence of a controlled dynamic reallocation mechanism, is not resolved by any reviewed solution.** In the reviewed systems, manual editing, last-minute change features and synchronization do not constitute controlled reallocation, because none of them treats the published seating map as a baseline state, none of them distinguishes affected participants from unaffected participants, and none of them minimizes disruption as an explicit objective. In the reviewed academic studies, the closest precedent is the warm-started local search of Ipsen et al. (2026), but that mechanism improves a solution within a single planning run rather than preserving a commitment that has already been published to participants. The techniques that do address this situation, which are the minimal perturbation problem (El Sakkout & Wallace, 2000) and large neighbourhood search (Shaw, 1998), together with the switching-cost objective structure of Hoang (2022), have not been applied to seating allocation in any reviewed work. In this project, reallocation is therefore defined as the incremental repair of the published seating map, in which the unaffected assignments are locked, only the affected assignments and a bounded surrounding neighbourhood are reconsidered, the number of unaffected participants moved is minimized ahead of the total movement distance, the repaired result is stored as a new version, and publication requires explicit approval. The intention is change optimization rather than mere change handling, and it will be evaluated by comparing the incremental repair against a full regeneration on the metrics defined for G2.1 and FR9.

**Problem Statement 3, which is the communication bottleneck in participant seat retrieval, is not resolved by any reviewed solution in the form that PJKIT requires.** Printed seating maps, PDF listings shared through WhatsApp and enquiries to event staff leave the participant without any assurance that they are reading the currently approved seating map. Among the reviewed systems, the event planning and seating chart tools offer only printing, exporting and sharing, which is not equivalent to an authenticated route onto the latest approved version, while the reserved-seat ticketing platforms do provide a per-attendee lookup but only in a model where the attendee selected the seat themselves at the point of purchase, which does not apply to an event where the administrator allocates seats by rule. The proposed participant-facing seat lookup is designed to provide a route on which a participant views only their own assignment, drawn only from the latest published version, and it will be evaluated against G3.1 and FR14.

**The nature of the gap and the boundary of the contribution.** The gap that this review establishes is a combination gap rather than a single missing feature. The individual capabilities exist separately across the market, since Eventbrite Reserved Seating gives an attendee the number of their own seat, Breeze ChMS holds the contribution amounts and the attendance history, the Vidyalaya exam seating module performs automatic rule-based allocation of numbered seats and can regenerate a plan after a change, and PerfectTablePlan performs automated optimization of guest placement. What no reviewed academic study or existing market system provides is the combination that the PJKIT use case requires, which is configurable participant-profile-based constraints, constraint-based optimization with falsifiable status reporting, controlled movement-minimizing repair of an already-published seating map, versioned publishing, and participant-facing lookup, all within a single workflow and over a venue whose structural rules include two-seat allocation units, demand-driven tier bands with shareable boundary rows, aisle-aware pairing and within-tier contribution ordering.

The academic contribution of this project lies in the first two (2) objectives, which are the modelling of the seating rules of a real community assembly as a configurable constraint optimization problem, including the multi-seat allocation units, the demand-driven tier bands, the aisle-aware adjacency and the within-tier contribution ordering, and the design of a stability-preserving incremental repair mechanism for seating maps that have already been published to the participants they affect. The participant-facing seat lookup in Objective 3 is a necessary system module that closes the operational loop by ensuring that participants read only approved and current seating information. It resolves Problem Statement 3 and it is not presented as an algorithmic contribution of this project.
