## Part A — Annotated Bibliography

### A.1 Seating and Spectator Allocation Literature

**Ipsen, A., Cashmore, M., Fielding, K., Marchesotti, N., Zehtabi, P., Magazzeni, D., & Veloso, M. (2026). *Beyond manual planning: Seating allocation for large organizations*. J.P. Morgan Chase AI Research. arXiv:2602.05875.**

This paper introduces the Hierarchical Seating Allocation Problem (HSAP), which extends a base Seating Allocation (SA) problem to organizations whose teams are nested in a reporting hierarchy (e.g., a Sales department containing EU Sales, US Sales, and Sales Engineering sub-teams). The authors decompose HSAP into a sequence of per-level SA sub-problems solved top-down via a depth-first traversal (the DF-HSA algorithm), each level's allocation constraining the seat pool available to the level below it. For the SA sub-problem itself, they propose four interchangeable solvers of increasing scalability and decreasing optimality guarantee: an exact Integer Program (IPSA) that selects a "central seat" per team and minimizes total distance from team members to it; an Iterative Clustering Algorithm (ICA/ICA++) resembling k-means, alternating a *location* step (recompute central seats) and an *allocation* step (assign seats to nearest central seat, solved as a smaller IP); a Greedy Seat Allocation (GSA) baseline using a regret-value heuristic; and a Local Search (LS) post-processing step that re-optimizes only the seats near each existing central seat, using the incumbent solution as a warm start. A second contribution addresses accurate distance estimation on real floor plans, replacing Euclidean/Manhattan distance (which underestimates cost across walls) with a probabilistic roadmap (PRM) built via rapidly-exploring random trees (RRT), then solved with Floyd–Warshall for all-pairs shortest paths.

*Relevance to this FYP.* HSAP's core insight — that within-tier optimality is cheap to compute exactly, but the full multi-level problem is not, so the model should be decomposed into tractable stages that are then locally repaired — parallels this project's own tier-zone structure (Emperor/Bodhi/Merit rows) and its planned local-search-style movement-minimization objective for dynamic reallocation [cf. §1.4.4–1.4.5, §3.3]. It also demonstrates empirically that a heuristic warm-started local search (their LS method) can materially improve an incumbent solution at low added runtime — direct precedent for this project's own plan to penalize seat movement relative to a previously published allocation rather than re-solve from scratch. Because the venue in this FYP is a single fixed floor plan with only three coarse tiers rather than a deep organizational hierarchy, and because CP-SAT is used as a single monolithic exact solver rather than a hierarchy of sub-problems, HSAP's decomposition strategy is more elaborate than what this project requires; nonetheless, its qualitative finding — that human-evaluated solution quality can diverge from the numerically optimized objective (their Figure 5 example, where an optimal PRM-distance solution still leaves a human decision-maker wanting to manually move two seats) — is a useful caution when this project defines its own soft-constraint weights and validates results independently, as CLAUDE.md already requires.

**Muñoz, V., Montaner, M., & López, B. (2005). Seat allocation for massive events based on region growing techniques. Universitat de Girona.**

Working from the case of the 2003 Formula 1 Grand Prix, this paper formulates spectator ticket-to-seat allocation as an anytime search problem. Tickets are grouped into *ticket groups* (TGs), further split into *ticket sub-groups* (TSs) when a group is too large to fit one contiguous block, each carrying a category, a priority rank, and dispersion/status flags. Seats similarly carry zone, row, column, category, rank, sector, and status attributes. Two classes of distribution rule are defined: mandatory rules (RM1–RM2: category and status must match between ticket and seat) and nine optional rules (RO1–RO9) that a human organizing committee would apply — subgroup splitting with margin/remainder, rank alignment between ticket and seat, minimum/maximum tickets per row, never leaving a single ticket isolated (RO5), avoiding empty seats at row edges (RO8), and enforcing an even *sparsity* of assigned seats across a zone so a partially sold venue still looks full (RO9). A composite fitness function (GF) aggregates a group-fitness term (based on rank alignment and the "joint fitness" of a sub-group's block shape versus an ideal rectangle), a sparsity term, and a heavy penalty for unassigned tickets. The solving method borrows *region growing* from computer-vision image segmentation: for each ticket sub-group, a seed seat is chosen (deterministically or randomly, depending on whether dispersion is required) and then "grown" outward by iteratively annexing the best-scoring neighboring seat until the sub-group is fully seated; unassignable sub-groups are deferred and later resolved by undoing nearby assignments to free contiguous space. A subsequent local-search pass improves the first candidate solution. The method is shown to scale to 50,000 tickets with an anytime property — a usable first solution in minutes, an improved solution obtainable if more time is allowed.

*Relevance to this FYP.* Several of the region-growing paper's optional rules map directly onto soft or hard constraints this FYP has already drafted or should consider adding: RM1/RM2 correspond to this project's participant-category-to-zone suitability and status-eligibility constraints [C5, C8 in Appendix A of the interim report]; RO2's subgroup splitting anticipates the project's group-adjacency soft constraint [C9] for cases where a family/delegation group cannot be seated contiguously; and RO5 ("never leave one ticket alone") together with RO8 ("avoid empty seats at row edges") are two concrete, previously unconsidered soft-constraint candidates that PJKIT's constraint listing (Appendix A) could usefully adopt to prevent isolated single-seat gaps in a row after allocation. The paper's sparsity rule (RO9) is not applicable to PJKIT, whose events are not undersold public ticketing and do not need to visually appear "fuller than it is." Methodologically, region growing is a constructive greedy-plus-local-search heuristic without any completeness or optimality guarantee and without an explicit hard/soft constraint separation solved via a general-purpose solver; it is included here as a literature comparison point for Table 2.2 rather than as a candidate implementation approach, since — per CLAUDE.md's mandate that a result must never be reported as optimal unless CP-SAT returns OPTIMAL — this project requires the provable-optimality and explicit solver-status semantics that CP-SAT provides and that a bespoke region-growing heuristic cannot.

**Sun, S. (2020). Mathematical model of seat arrangement in large gymnasium. *IOP Conference Series: Materials Science and Engineering*, *806*, 012013. https://doi.org/10.1088/1757-899X/806/1/012013**

This paper is not an allocation-optimization paper but a combinatorics/sequence paper: it models how the *number of seats per row* grows down a stadium stand when rows are periodically interrupted by access aisles. The author defines an "arithmetic sequence of periodical arithmetic jump" — within each block of *t* rows the seat count increases by a constant intra-period difference *d* (reflecting the stand's rake), but at every *t*-th row (an aisle) the count instead jumps by a separate jump difference *e*. Closed-form formulas are derived for the *n*-th term and for the cumulative seat total across *n* rows, and a worked example computes the total capacity of a 72-row stand with an aisle every 13 rows. A geometric-sequence analogue is also sketched for cases where row width grows multiplicatively rather than additively.

*Relevance to this FYP.* This paper is best read as a floor-plan generation reference rather than an allocation-optimization reference. Its aisle-periodicity model is structurally identical to what this project's `floor_plan_generator` must encode: a fixed number of seats per row with periodic breaks, and — more specifically to this FYP's Emperor pairing rule — the requirement that positions 6 and 7 in a row are separated by a centre aisle and can never form a valid Emperor pair (per CLAUDE.md's Gotchas). Where Sun's model treats seat count as the variable that jumps at an aisle, this project's floor plan is simpler (a fixed 12-seat row with one central aisle, not a variable per-row count), so the general periodic-jump formula is not needed in full; however, it is a useful citation to justify, in mathematical terms, why aisle position is treated as a structural break in physical adjacency rather than an ordinary seat-to-seat gap — directly supporting the "physical adjacency must use `physical_position`, not `priority_rank`" rule already documented in CLAUDE.md's Gotchas.

### A.2 Constraint-Solving Foundations Relevant to the Chosen Technology

**Hoang, K. D. (2022). *Dynamic continuous distributed constraint optimization problems* [Doctoral dissertation, Washington University in St. Louis].**

This dissertation is not about seating at all; it is included because it provides the formal Distributed Constraint Optimization Problem (DCOP) vocabulary and hard/soft, weighted-objective constraint-optimization framing that underlies the constraint-programming paradigm this FYP adopts, and because two of its three contributions are directly analogous to problems this FYP must solve. A DCOP is defined as a tuple of agents, decision variables with finite domains, utility (constraint) functions, and an assignment function; the goal is an assignment maximizing the sum of applicable utility function values — precisely the "maximize objective subject to constraints" structure of a Constraint Optimization Problem that this FYP's Chapter 2 already invokes generically via Rossi et al. (2006). The dissertation's first strand, Proactive Dynamic DCOPs (PD-DCOPs), models a sequence of problems that change over discrete time steps, adding an explicit *switching cost* term to the objective that penalizes changing a decision variable's value between consecutive time steps, and proves error bounds relating solution quality to the planning horizon and to how far in advance changes are known. Its second strand, Continuous DCOPs, extends the discrete-domain assumption to continuous variables, introducing exact and approximate solving methods (extensions of the Distributed Pseudo-tree Optimization Procedure, DPOP) with proven error bounds — relevant background on why domains and utility functions must be handled carefully when the "seat" search space is treated as a numeric one, though not directly applicable to this FYP since seats here are a finite discrete set.

*Relevance to this FYP.* The PD-DCOP objective structure — a per-time-step utility term minus a weighted switching-cost term summed over a finite horizon — is structurally identical to the reallocation objective this project must implement for dynamic/on-event-day reallocation [§1.4.5, Objective 2, Appendix A rule C10]: PJKIT's "regenerate the plan while minimizing unnecessary seat movement relative to the previously published plan" is a single-step (horizon-of-one) special case of PD-DCOP's more general multi-time-step switching-cost objective. This gives the movement-minimization soft constraint already drafted in this project a named, formally studied counterpart in the DCOP literature, and its theoretical error-bound results (e.g., that the achievable solution-quality loss from ignoring future changes is bounded in terms of the maximum per-step utility swing) offer a template this project could cite when justifying, in the final report, why penalizing movement in a single-shot re-solve is a reasonable and boundedly-suboptimal simplification rather than an ad hoc heuristic. The dissertation's *distributed* framing — multiple autonomous agents, each controlling one variable, communicating via message passing (DPOP, MGM, DSA) — is not applicable to this FYP, whose engine is a single centralized CP-SAT solve inside one AWS Lambda invocation rather than a multi-agent negotiation; this distinction should be noted explicitly in the report so the citation is not misread as implying this project uses distributed/multi-agent solving.

**Google. (n.d.). *CP-SAT solver*. Google for Developers — OR-Tools. https://developers.google.com/optimization/cp/cp_solver**

The official vendor documentation for the solver this project adopts (already cited in the interim report as Google, n.d.-a). It states that CP-SAT is an integer-only constraint programming solver — all constraints and objective coefficients must be integers, which is why CLAUDE.md mandates that "CP-SAT does not accept floating-point objective coefficients" and that every cost be scaled to an integer before model construction — and documents the solver's status values (OPTIMAL, FEASIBLE, INFEASIBLE, MODEL_INVALID, UNKNOWN) that this project's engine must surface as part of its quality metrics [§1.4.1, §3.2.2 Figure 3.8].

**Perron, L., & Didier, F. (2023). The CP-SAT-LP solver (invited talk). In *Proceedings of the 29th International Conference on Principles and Practice of Constraint Programming (CP 2023)* (Article 3). Schloss Dagstuhl – Leibniz-Zentrum für Informatik. https://doi.org/10.4230/LIPIcs.CP.2023.3**

Authored by the Google OR-Tools team itself, this peer-reviewed invited talk paper is the primary technical source (rather than developer documentation) for how CP-SAT actually works: it is built on the lazy clause generation (LCG) paradigm — a hybrid of Boolean satisfiability (SAT) solving and constraint propagation, inspired by the Chuffed solver — extended with an integrated linear-programming (LP) relaxation layer and a portfolio of diverse parallel search workers. Constraint propagation over integer domains is combined with conflict-driven clause learning (CDCL) from SAT solving, so that when a propagation fails, the solver learns a reusable "no-good" clause rather than simply backtracking, and the LP relaxation supplies tighter bounds that prune the search further.

*Relevance to this FYP.* This paper upgrades the technology-selection justification in Section 3.3 of the interim report from a citation of vendor documentation alone to a citation of the primary peer-reviewed source describing the solver's internal architecture, giving a more defensible academic basis for the claim that CP-SAT is "strong for hard constraints, soft-constraint penalties, if-then rules, and solver status reporting" [Table 2.2]. It also substantiates, in technical terms, why CP-SAT can reliably return a provable OPTIMAL status on problems of this project's scale (on the order of 100–200 participants and seats) even though the underlying seat-assignment problem is combinatorially large: the CDCL-plus-LP-relaxation-plus-portfolio-search architecture is specifically designed to prune such search spaces far more aggressively than naive backtracking or the greedy/GA baselines compared in Table 2.2.

**Stuckey, P. J. (2010). Lazy clause generation: Combining the power of SAT and CP (and MIP?) solving. In A. Lodi, M. Milano, & P. Toth (Eds.), *Integration of AI and OR Techniques in Constraint Programming for Combinatorial Optimization Problems* (CPAIOR 2010, LNCS 6140, pp. 5–9). Springer. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-13520-0_3**

This is the foundational paper (referenced directly by Perron & Didier, 2023, as CP-SAT's inspiration) introducing lazy clause generation: rather than pre-compiling a constraint's full SAT encoding up front, the solver represents integer variables and constraints natively and only generates the SAT clauses that explain a given propagation or conflict "lazily," on demand, combining the compactness and semantics of constraint-programming propagators with the conflict-driven learning power of modern SAT solvers.

*Relevance to this FYP.* Included as the primary theoretical citation underpinning both CP-SAT itself and, transitively, this project's central technology choice; it is the correct citation to use if the final report's methodology or theory chapter wishes to explain *why* a lazy-clause-generation-based solver like CP-SAT can express the tier-zone capacity, Emperor-pair, and priority-ordering hard constraints natively as integer/Boolean propagators rather than requiring them to be hand-compiled into flat SAT clauses.

## Part B — Synthesized Literature Review Narrative (for insertion into Chapter 2)

The academic literature on seating and spectator allocation, though thin relative to general combinatorial optimization, converges on the same structural decomposition this FYP has already adopted independently: a hard-constraint layer that guarantees validity (one occupant per seat, category/status eligibility, blocked or unavailable seats excluded) and a soft, weighted layer that expresses preference (proximity to a reference point, rank alignment, contiguity, and — critically for this project — stability of assignment across successive plan versions). Ipsen et al. (2026) formalize this decomposition for organizational hierarchies and demonstrate that even where an exact integer program is available, a warm-started local-search repair step is the practical mechanism for keeping later re-solves close to an existing incumbent — direct precedent for how this project's dynamic-reallocation objective should be framed as a movement-penalized re-optimization rather than a from-scratch re-solve. Muñoz et al. (2005), working in the very different setting of Formula 1 spectator ticketing, independently arrive at several of the same soft-constraint categories this project has drafted (category/status matching, group contiguity) and additionally surface two soft-constraint patterns — never leaving a single seat isolated in a row, and avoiding vacant seats at row edges — that PJKIT's current constraint listing (Appendix A of the interim report) does not yet include and that could reasonably be proposed as additional weighted soft constraints during the requirement-validation cycle already under way with the industry collaborator [§3.4]. Sun (2020) contributes not an allocation method but a combinatorial model of aisle-interrupted row geometry, corroborating, from a distinct mathematical angle, this project's existing treatment of the central aisle as a hard structural break in physical adjacency rather than an ordinary seat gap.

Notably, none of the three seating-specific papers uses a general-purpose constraint-programming solver with a provable optimality guarantee: Ipsen et al. rely on bespoke integer programs and clustering heuristics with explicit scalability/optimality trade-offs, and Muñoz et al. rely on a constructive computer-vision-inspired heuristic with no completeness guarantee at all. This is the gap the present FYP occupies and the reason Table 2.2's method comparison favors CP-SAT: unlike either published approach, CP-SAT can express PJKIT's tier-zone, Emperor-pair, and contribution-ordering rules declaratively as integer and Boolean constraints, optimize a single weighted objective over all soft preferences simultaneously, and report an explicit, falsifiable solver status (OPTIMAL, FEASIBLE, INFEASIBLE) — a property CLAUDE.md's own engineering rules already treat as non-negotiable ("never report a solution as optimal unless CP-SAT returns OPTIMAL"). Hoang's (2022) dissertation, while addressing a distributed multi-agent setting this project does not require, supplies the closest formal precedent for the *specific* reallocation objective this FYP must implement: its Proactive Dynamic DCOP model treats stability across time steps as a first-class, weighted term in the objective function alongside per-step utility, exactly mirroring the "minimize movement relative to the previous published plan" requirement of Objective 2. Finally, the primary technical literature on CP-SAT itself (Stuckey, 2010; Perron & Didier, 2023) explains, at the level of solver internals rather than vendor marketing, why a lazy-clause-generation solver augmented with linear-programming bounds and portfolio search is well matched to a problem of this project's scale — reinforcing, from a peer-reviewed rather than purely commercial source, the technology-selection argument already advanced in Section 3.3.
